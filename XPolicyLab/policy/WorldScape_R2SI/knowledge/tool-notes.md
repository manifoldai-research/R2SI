## The robot
World metres: x across the table (left arm at negative x), y away from the robot, z up. The usual table surface is at z = 0.765; check the surface you work on. The arm bases stand at (-0.30, -0.45) and (0.30, -0.45). Measure object heights; do not guess them.

A position is the grasp point, the centre of the gripping pads, 0.15 m ahead of the flange. An orientation is a wxyz quaternion of the flange: its +x is the direction the fingers point, its +y the direction they close along. robo.hand(pointing, closing) builds one, robo.turned rotates one. To turn what a hand holds by more than a quarter turn about the vertical, use robo.turn about its grasp point and read degrees_turned: an orientation given to move or place can be taken half a turn away when the hand took its load half turned, and the load then lands reversed. The pads are 1.5 cm deep, centred on the grasp point, and end at the fingertips about 8 mm past it, so with a hand pointing straight down the fingertips touch the surface when the grasp point is about 8 mm above it. To take something thin, stop just above that: the pads then close as low as they can. A fully open jaw spans 8.8 cm.

Reach: each arm works best on its own side and can pass the middle only so far. Measured along the table from the arm's base to the grasp point, a hand straight down reaches about 0.50 m at z = 0.80, 0.46 at 0.90 and 0.37 at 1.00, and not above about 1.08. Leaning 30 degrees away from the base adds about 0.15 m, leaning 45 about 0.20. A level hand reaches about 0.75 m, but not nearer than 0.48 m at z = 0.80 or 0.25 m at 0.90. Above about 1.1 m the arm has to fold over itself, so look and carry below that where you can. robo.reach answers this at 0 steps, for the robot alone. A free motion goes up and over what robo.locate has measured when its path would run into it, and is refused, naming the object, when no way over can be planned. It knows nothing of what has not been measured or has moved since, so measure what is around before you move through it, and measure again what has moved. Thin parts standing up from an object, such as flaps, a lid or a handle, are not in its measured height: locate them as objects of their own, and again after the object moves. Straight motions do not avoid anything. Near objects, lift first, travel, then descend straight.

## Cells
Each python call runs one cell in a namespace that persists for the whole episode; use variables for what you have learned. numpy is `np`, math is `math`; json, itertools, functools, statistics and collections can be imported. There is no file access; no classes, `with`, `finally`, `getattr` or attribute assignment. A cell may compute for up to a minute of Python time.

A motion that fails raises RoboFailure and ends the cell, so later lines do not run on a false assumption; the cell's result shows what happened. Pass check=False to get the result dict instead and decide in code, or catch RoboFailure. Print what you need to see: only what you print, the last expression, and a summary of the robo calls come back, with fresh images after any motion. Use robo.show for anything more.

Write a cell for one coherent stretch of work with its checks in it: measure, act, verify, continue or stop. When a program can decide a step from measurements, let it. When the situation is new or something surprised you, stop the cell and look. Loops that measure, shift and measure again are how fine alignment gets done, and they cost only the steps they move. When one item has worked, put what you did into a function and call it for the next, changing only the target.

## Seeing and measuring
Start from the first images: name what you see, then robo.locate each thing with a short visual phrase of what it looks like. If a phrase finds nothing, try its shape and colour; a broad phrase like "object" lists most of what is on the table; or find it by eye with robo.show and use robo.locate_at. Check identity: robo.show(objects=[...]) draws what was found. Segmentation finds a thing that looks like the phrase, not necessarily the one you mean; words like "left" do not select instances, so choose among results by position and color yourself.

One camera does not fix height. `cameras` and `agreement` in a result tell you what you have. From the initial pose the wrist cameras see only the near part of the table, so far objects are usually seen by the head camera alone; such a measurement is good enough to go near, not to grasp by. robo.pick measures again from the wrist on its way down. For anything else, robo.look brings a wrist camera about 20 cm from the thing, looking straight at it, and you measure there; do this on the way to somewhere you are going anyway.

Measurements go stale. After any contact, or any motion of an object, measure again; never replay old coordinates. A low score, or a position that jumps, usually means an arm is in the way: move it and measure again. For an object standing on another, give on=<its surface height>, and measure the surface you will build on each time.

For small features use a phrase for the part, or mark the feature in two cameras with robo.triangulate; a large gap means the pixels are not the same point. Do the same for anything in the air, as what a hand holds: robo.locate takes things to stand on a surface, so mark the points you need, such as a rim, in two cameras. Enlarge with robo.show(region=..., grid=True) before reading pixels. robo.project shows where a world point falls, so you can check a target against the image before moving. To test at no cost whether a measured shape fits a place, lay its outline (robo.footprint) at that position and turn on a close image (robo.project, robo.show with outline). Aligned from far above is not aligned at contact height: look again close to the goal, and from a second direction for tight fits. The wrist camera of a free hand gives that direction, and sees what the working hand hides: robo.look with direction asks for the side.

## When something fails
`failure` says what happened. `unreachable`: the plan was refused at 0 steps, with a reason, and nothing moved. Do not resend it; change the orientation, arm, approach or travel height, using robo.reach. `interrupted`: the arm stopped following, so something blocks it or it hit something: look before anything else, as the object may have moved. `contact`: the arm is blocked in a contact stage. `not_holding`: see Grasping.

If an object was dropped, stop carrying nothing to the destination. Find it from where it last was and the direction it went. If progress stalls, change your hypothesis, not the amount of force: another contact point, direction or arm. Repeating an action changes little, and a failure that repeats means something you believe is wrong.
